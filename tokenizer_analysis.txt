1. VOCABULARY SIZE
Given the stats below, it seems like the only real difference between the character-level
tokenizer and the small BPE tokenizer is the whitespace token, which was ignored for the
BPE tokenizers due to Whitespace pre-tokenization. Otherwise, both contain literally every
character in the training set. The big BPE tokenizer, on the other hand, also adds a few
merges (since the vocab_size limit was 10,000 > 9,446). The number of merges is still quite
low, however, since there's not much space left after accounting for all the 9,443
characters in the training set.

------------------vocab_size------------------
            |    en    |    tr    |    zh    |
Char-level  |      9447|      9447|      9447|
Small BPE   |      9446|      9446|      9446|
Big BPE     |     10000|     10000|     10000|


2. AVERAGE UNICODE CHARACTERS PER TOKEN
Again, the character-level tokenizer and the small BPE tokenizer are basically identical:
the difference is just the inclusion of a whitespace token. As mentioned above, the big BPE
tokenizer actually does include some actual merges, which is why it pushes the stat to 1.1 

------------avg_unicode_per_token-------------
            |    en    |    tr    |    zh    |
Char-level  |    1.0022|    1.0022|    1.0022|
Small BPE   |    1.0022|    1.0022|    1.0022|
Big BPE     |       1.1|       1.1|       1.1|


3. TOTAL TOKENS
As expected, the character-level tokenizer results in the most tokens, with the small BPE
model trailing only slightly behind. In comparison, the big BPE tokenizer creates about half
the tokens for English and Turkish. For Chinese, however, the improvement is only marginal,
which is expected since there are so many Chinese characters and since each character contains
so much meaning.

-----------------total_tokens-----------------
            |    en    |    tr    |    zh    |
Char-level  |3.6827e+05|3.6838e+05|3.6827e+05|
Small BPE   |3.0911e+05|3.2346e+05|3.6286e+05|
Big BPE     |1.6223e+05|1.6766e+05|3.3537e+05|


4. AVERAGE TOKENS PER SENTENCE
Here, the few BPE merges for Chinese means that the average token per sentence is actually
comparable for all three tokenizers. BPE clearly favors English more, with Turkish being 
a kind of middle ground. As expected, average tokens per sentence goes down for all languages
when going from character-level to small BPE to big BPE, since especially the big BPE tokenizer
can use bigger tokens.

-------------avg_tokens_per_sent--------------
            |    en    |    tr    |    zh    |
Char-level  |    81.117|     98.63|    38.546|
Small BPE   |    68.086|    86.602|     37.98|
Big BPE     |    35.734|     44.89|    35.103|


5. PATTERNS FROM SAMPLE SENTENCES
Below are the different tokenizations for nine sample sentences: three for each language. The BPE
tokenizers lack blank space due to the Whitespace pre-tokenizer in the transformer library. 
Otherwise, the small BPE tokenization is virtually identical to the char-level tokenization,
regardless of language.

The big BPE tokenization provides more interesting results. Several common English words -- 
including fairly long ones like "which" and "that" -- have received their own tokens, just like
common affixes (e.g. "ation" and "ing"). Without knowing Turkish, I suppose the same is true
there except perhaps to a smaller degree: we see e.g. the word "alan" and the long suffixes
"asında" and "mıştır". As for Chinese, the only BPE merges I can see actually relate to numbers
("14") and Western characters ("ord") rather than Chinese characters.

I guess I am a bit surprised that not more punctuation marks were included in merges. I also
find it interesting that some seemingly arbitrary number combinations "make it" (19, 50),
whereas others do not (7 8, 7 0). Investigating which number combinations are more likely to
become merged would be an interesting study. Something else that could be interesting to 
study would be how much "help" e.g. Turkish gets from English merges, and vice versa.
